Every grading decision — from a $12 Pikachu to a five-figure Charizard — comes down to one formula. Learn it once and you'll evaluate any card in under a minute, with no tools at all.

## The formula

**Profit = (Expected graded sale price) − (Raw sale price) − (All-in grading cost)**

That's it. Three inputs, one subtraction chain. Let's define each honestly:

- **Expected graded sale price:** not the PSA 10 price — the price at the grade you *realistically expect*. Use recent *sold* listings (not asking prices) for that exact card in that grade.
- **Raw sale price:** what your card would sell for today, ungraded. If you'd never sell it raw, use what you'd have to pay to replace it.
- **All-in grading cost:** tier fee + your share of shipping both ways + supplies (Card Saver, team bag, box). Budget **~$85** for a Standard-tier card in a small batch; ~$63–70 in a 20+ card batch.

## The break-even sale price

Rearrange the formula and you get the single most useful number in grading:

**Break-even = Raw price + All-in cost**

This is the minimum your graded card must sell for to not lose money. A $90 raw card at $85 all-in needs a **$175** graded sale to break even. Now look at reality: if PSA 9s of that card sell for $155, you already know — before spending a cent — that anything short of a 10 loses money.

Memorize this: **if the PSA 9 price is below break-even, you're betting the entire $85 on a gem mint.** Sometimes that's a fine bet. Usually it isn't.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 — the easy yes.** Modern alt-art, pack-fresh:
- Raw: $380 | PSA 10: $5,200 | All-in: $85
- Profit at 10: $5,200 − $380 − $85 = **+$4,735**
- Even a PSA 9 ($1,400) profits: $1,400 − $380 − $85 = **+$935**
- Verdict: grade it. The margin survives a 9.

**Example 2 — the trap.** Hyped modern card, soft corners:
- Raw: $700 | PSA 10: $1,400 | PSA 9: $850 | All-in: $85
- Profit at 10: +$615. Profit at 9: **−$35**... wait: $850 − $700 − $85 = **+$65**. Thin.
- Verdict: only if it's a genuine 10 candidate. A likely 9 makes $65 for 4+ months of waiting — pass.

**Example 3 — the hard no.** Base set common in great shape:
- Raw: $12 | PSA 10: $210 | All-in: $85
- Profit at 10: $210 − $12 − $85 = **+$113**
- Looks profitable! But: pop report shows 15,000 PSA 10s, the card is abundant, and one soft corner makes it a $45 PSA 9 — a $52 loss. Verdict: the *expected value* is negative once you weight by grade probability. Which brings us to...

## Expected value: the pro move

Amateurs compute profit at PSA 10. Professionals compute **expected value**:

**EV = (P₁₀ × Profit₁₀) + (P₉ × Profit₉) + (P₈ × Profit₈) − ...**

Where P₁₀ is your honest probability of each grade. Example 3 with realistic probabilities:

- 20% PSA 10 (+$113) + 50% PSA 9 (−$52) + 30% PSA 8 or lower (−$80+)
- EV = $22.60 − $26 − $24 = **−$27.40**

Negative expected value — skip it, even though "PSA 10 profits $113." This is the mistake that separates profitable submitters from everyone else: they price in their *own* grading accuracy.

## The slab multiplier shortcut

When you're scanning quickly, use the **slab multiplier**: PSA 10 price ÷ raw price.

- **5x+**: almost always worth a serious look
- **2.5–5x**: worth it if the card is clean and the dollar upside clears $100
- **Under 2.5x**: rarely worth it at 2026 tier prices — the $85 floor eats the margin

Combine the multiplier with the break-even check and you can triage a 50-card stack in ten minutes.

## Building your own grading spreadsheet

If you submit regularly, build a simple tracker. Columns: card name, set, raw price, PSA 9 price, PSA 10 price, expected grade, all-in cost, expected profit, actual grade received, actual sale price. After 20–30 submissions, two numbers emerge that change everything:

1. **Your personal 10-rate.** Most collectors wildly overestimate this. If yours is 25%, every future calculation should weight outcomes accordingly.
2. **Your average profit per submission.** If it's negative, the problem isn't the cards — it's the filter. Tighten it.

This spreadsheet is the highest-ROI document in your grading operation. It converts vibes into data and makes the break-even formula personal instead of theoretical.

## Common math mistakes

**Using asking prices instead of sold prices.** eBay asking prices are fantasies; sold listings are facts. A card "listed" at $900 with recent solds at $640 has a $640 comp. Always filter to sold/completed listings.

**Forgetting the selling fees.** eBay takes ~13%, PayPal/payment processing takes its cut. A $720 PSA 10 sale nets you roughly $620 after fees. If your profit calc used $720, you overstated by $100. Factor ~13–15% selling fees into every calculation — or sell via lower-fee venues and note the difference.

**Ignoring the time value.** $475 profit in 5 months isn't the same as $475 today. For flippers, annualize it: that $475 on $505 of locked capital (card + grading) over 5 months is roughly a 200%+ annualized return — still excellent. But a $65 profit on the same lock-up is ~30% annualized before risk. Suddenly "profitable" looks thin. Always ask: profit *per month of waiting*.

**Counting the raw price at what you paid, not what it's worth.** If you pulled the card from a pack three years ago, your cost basis is $0 — but the formula needs the *opportunity cost*: what you could sell it for raw today. Using $0 makes everything look profitable and teaches you nothing. Use current raw market value, always.
