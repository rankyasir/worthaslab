Every collector has stood in front of a binder and thought: *should I grade this?* In 2026, it's a harder question than ever. PSA's cheapest tier is $59.99, Value tiers are paused, and all-in costs run ~$85 per card. Get it right and a $90 raw card becomes a $720 PSA 10. Get it wrong and you've paid $85 to own a $155 PSA 9.

This guide gives you the decision framework professionals use.

## When grading is worth it

Grading pays when **three conditions** line up:

1. **The PSA 10 premium is large.** You're not paying for the grade — you're paying for the *multiplier*. A card that goes from $90 raw to $720 at PSA 10 (8x) is a grading candidate. A card that goes from $90 raw to $140 at PSA 10 is not.
2. **The card has a real shot at a 10.** Centering within PSA's tolerances, sharp corners, clean edges, flawless surface. If you're honest and it's a likely 9, run the math on the 9 price, not the 10 price.
3. **The all-in cost leaves meat on the bone.** After ~$85 in grading + shipping + supplies, plus months of waiting, the remaining profit has to be worth it. Most experienced submitters want $100+ of expected upside.

Miss any one of the three and the answer is usually no.

## When grading is NOT worth it

- **The card is worth under ~$40 raw.** At $59.99+ per slab, the math almost never works. There are exceptions (low-pop vintage where even a PSA 8 commands a premium), but they're exceptions.
- **It's for your personal collection and you'll never sell.** Grading is authentication + liquidity. If neither matters to you, a $5 magnetic holder does the job.
- **The PSA 9 price is below your break-even.** This is the silent killer. Most submitted cards grade 9, not 10. If (PSA 9 price − raw − $85) is negative, you're betting everything on a 10. Sometimes that's fine — just know you're gambling, not investing.
- **You need the money soon.** Standard tier takes 90–100 business days. That's 4–5 calendar months where your card and your $85 are locked up.

## The math, worked end to end

Let's take a real pattern — a modern alt-art Pokémon card:

- Raw recent sold average: **$420**
- PSA 9 recent sold average: **$520**
- PSA 10 recent sold average: **$980**
- All-in grading cost (Standard + shipping share): **$85**

Outcomes:
- **PSA 10:** $980 − $420 − $85 = **+$475** ✓
- **PSA 9:** $520 − $420 − $85 = **+$15** (basically break-even)
- **PSA 8:** almost certainly a loss

## The psychology trap

Here's what actually burns collectors: they do the math on the PSA 10 price while *looking* at a PSA 9 card. We all overgrade our own cards — it's human. Professional submitters use a simple discipline: **grade the card in front of you, not the card you wish it was.** Check centering with a real tool (not your eyes), tilt for surface scratches under a lamp, and look at corners with magnification. Then run the numbers on the grade you *expect*, with the 10 as upside.

## What about CGC, BGS, SGC?

PSA commands the highest resale premiums in most categories — that's why this guide (and most of the market) defaults to it. CGC is growing fast (up 631% by some measures) and is gaining ground, especially where collectors want a credible PSA alternative. But if your goal is maximizing resale value per dollar of grading cost, PSA 10 remains the benchmark everything else is measured against. The math in this guide works for any grader — just swap in that company's fees and that company's realized prices.

## The bottom line

Is PSA grading worth it in 2026? **Sometimes — and the sometimes is quantifiable.** Cards with a 2.5x+ slab multiplier, a genuine shot at gem mint, and $100+ of expected upside after ~$85 in costs: grade them all day. Everything else: keep it raw, sell it raw, or buy it already graded from someone whose math worked better than yours.

Run every candidate through the calculator before you submit. Five seconds now beats $85 of regret in five months.

## Three collector case studies

**Case 1: The disciplined flipper.** Maria submits 4 times a year, 15–20 cards per batch, all at Standard tier. Her rule: only cards with 3x+ multipliers and $150+ expected upside. Her hit rate on 10s is ~35% — slightly above average because she pre-screens ruthlessly. Annual grading spend: ~$5,500. Annual profit after all costs: ~$14,000. Her secret isn't grading skill; it's *not submitting* the other 60 cards she considered.

**Case 2: The curiosity grader.** James grades whatever excites him that month — 3–5 cards, Express tier because he's impatient. Average all-in: $225 per card. Half come back 9s on cards where the math only worked at 10. Annual grading spend: ~$8,000. Annual profit: roughly −$1,500. He loves the hobby and can afford it — but he's funding it, not profiting from it.

**Case 3: The PC collector.** Aisha grades only cards she'll never sell — her favorite alt arts, her childhood Base Set holos. She knows the math doesn't "work" as an investment and doesn't care; the $85 is entertainment spend, like a nice dinner. Annual spend: ~$1,000. Regret: zero.

All three are rational *if they're honest about which game they're playing*. The losses happen when Case 2 collectors believe they're Case 1.

## The 2026 market context

Zoom out and the timing is genuinely interesting. The grading market is valued at $4.5 billion in 2026, projected to nearly quadruple by 2036. Pokémon has structurally overtaken sports cards — TCG up 97% in 2025 while sports declined. That means the deepest, most liquid grading market is also the most competitive: everyone is grading Charizards, so Charizard premiums compress.

The edge in 2026 isn't in what everyone grades — it's in selectivity, condition assessment, and cost discipline. The collectors thriving aren't submitting more cards than they did in 2021. They're submitting *better* cards, at the cheapest tier that fits, with the math done beforehand. That's the entire game now.
