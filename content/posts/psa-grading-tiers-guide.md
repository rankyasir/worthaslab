Picking the wrong PSA tier is one of the most expensive unforced errors in the hobby. Go too cheap and your card sits for five months. Go too pricey and you've donated $140 for impatience. Here's how each 2026 tier actually works and how to choose.

## The tier list (2026)

| Tier | Fee | Max declared value | Turnaround |
|---|---|---|---|
| Standard | $59.99 | $1,000 | 90–100 business days |
| Priority | $79.99 | $1,500 | 70–80 business days |
| Express | $199 | $2,500 | 20–30 business days |
| Super Express | $349 | $5,000 | 10–15 business days |
| Premier | $599 | $10,000 | 7–10 business days |
| Premium 1 | $999 | $25,000 | 5–7 business days |

Two variables set the price: **speed** and **declared value cap**. The cap is the critical one — every tier has a maximum declared value, and if PSA judges your card worth more than the cap, they bump the card up and bill you the difference. This is not negotiable and not worth fighting: declare honestly.

Note the gap in the lineup: the Value tiers ($24.99–$64.99) were paused in June 2026 and haven't returned. Standard at $59.99 is the cheapest way in the door.

## How to choose: the decision tree

**Step 1: What's the card worth?** Find recent *sold* prices for the card in the grade you expect. That number picks your minimum tier — a $1,200 card cannot go Standard ($1,000 cap); it needs Priority minimum.

**Step 2: Does speed matter?** For 95% of cards, no. Card prices move, but a $400 card isn't going to collapse in four months — and if you think it might, that's information: maybe don't grade a depreciating asset at all. Pay for speed only when:
- The card is tied to a **time-sensitive event** (playoff run, movie release, set hype window)
- It's a **flip**, not a hold — your capital is locked while PSA has the card
- The price difference between tiers is small relative to the card's value

**Step 3: Sanity-check the fee against the upside.** Express costs $199 vs Standard's $59.99 — a $139 premium. If your expected profit at PSA 10 is $600, that premium is 23% of your profit gone for ~70 fewer business days of waiting. Usually a bad trade. If your expected profit is $4,000, the $139 is noise and getting the card back sooner has real option value.

## The mistakes everyone makes

**Mistake 1: Express for impatience.** The most common waste in grading. Collectors pay $199 because waiting is boring, not because the card needs speed. Unless you can articulate *why* 60 days matters in dollars, choose Standard.

**Mistake 2: Under-declaring value to sneak into a cheaper tier.** PSA checks declared values at intake. They will bump the card and charge you — and now you've also slowed your own order down with the adjustment.

**Mistake 3: Over-declaring "to be safe."** Declared value also sets your insurance coverage. Declaring a $300 card at $900 doesn't buy safety; it just pushes you toward a tier you don't need. Declare the honest market value.

**Mistake 4: Ignoring turnaround in the ROI math.** Five months is real time. If you're grading a modern card whose price decays 5% a month after set hype fades, a $1,000 PSA 10 today might be an $800 PSA 10 when it comes back. Factor decay into expected profit — or choose a faster tier deliberately, with eyes open.

## Tier cheat sheet by card value

- **Under $200 expected value:** Standard. Always. The fee is already a big fraction of upside.
- **$200–$800:** Standard, unless time-sensitive. Priority only if the card is near the $1,000 cap.
- **$800–$2,500:** Priority or Express. Do the fee-vs-upside math explicitly.
- **$2,500+:** Super Express and up. At these values, insurance, speed, and PSA's handling priority start mattering — and the fee is a small fraction of the card's value.

## The bottom line

For most collectors, most of the time: **Standard tier, honest declared value, patience.** The tiers above exist for genuinely time-sensitive or high-value cards, not for feelings. ## Declared value: the deep dive

Declared value does three jobs at once, and collectors routinely misunderstand all three:

1. **Tier eligibility.** It must sit under your tier's cap. This is the binding constraint — get it wrong and PSA bumps your tier.
2. **Insurance coverage.** If your card is lost or damaged in PSA's custody, declared value is the basis for your claim. Under-declaring to sneak into a cheaper tier doesn't just risk a bump — it under-insures a valuable card. Don't do it.
3. **Upcharge trigger.** If PSA believes the card is worth *more* than you declared — beyond the tier cap — the upcharge applies. This most often bites on cards that spike *after* you submit but before grading. You declared at $800; by grading time it's a $1,600 card. Surprise bill.

**How to declare:** use the recent sold price at your *expected* grade, rounded sensibly. Not the PSA 10 price if you're expecting a 9. Not last year's price if the market moved. Current comps, expected grade, honest number. If the card sits near a tier boundary (say $950 on a $1,000-cap Standard tier), consider whether Priority's higher cap is worth the $20 for peace of mind — upcharge surprises are worse than planned spending.

## Tier changes to watch

PSA's tier structure isn't static — 2026 already saw the Value pause and the new Standard tier. What to monitor:

- **Value tier return.** PSA has tied it to backlog levels (~5M cards). If it happens, sub-$50 grading returns and the entire break-even math shifts down — more cards become submittable overnight.
- **Fee adjustments.** Express rose from $149 to $199 in the 2026 reshuffle. Tiers get repriced; the direction has historically been up at the fast end.
- **New tiers.** PSA occasionally adds limited-time or category-specific tiers. They're usually worth ignoring unless they clearly beat your current option on fee-for-speed.

Set a calendar reminder to re-check PSA's published pricing quarterly. Your spreadsheet's cost assumptions should never be more than a few months old — stale tier data is how $85 estimates quietly become $110 realities.
