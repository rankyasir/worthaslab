Pokémon isn't just part of the grading boom — it *is* the grading boom. In 2026, trading card games made up 71% of PSA's record 2.5-million-card months, up 95% year over year. If you're holding Pokémon cards, you're holding the most-graded cardboard on earth.

But here's the uncomfortable truth: **most Pokémon cards should never be graded.** The sets are printed in massive quantities, PSA 10 populations are enormous for common cards, and at $59.99+ per slab the math only works for a select few. This guide shows you how to find them.

## The Pokémon grading reality

Modern Pokémon sets (Sword & Shield era onward) are printed at staggering scale. That Charizard you pulled? Thousands of others pulled it too, and hundreds have already graded it. High PSA 10 populations crush premiums — a card with 20,000 PSA 10s doesn't command the multiplier of a card with 200.

That doesn't mean grading Pokémon is dead. It means **selectivity is everything.** The winners share traits:

- **Alt arts and special illustration rares** — the market pays real premiums for these at PSA 10
- **Low-population 10s relative to demand** — check the pop report before submitting
- **Pack-fresh condition** — modern print quality is good, so 10s are achievable, but only from truly clean copies
- **Strong raw-to-10 multiplier** — 2.5x minimum, 5x+ ideally

## The Pokémon-specific checklist

Before submitting any Pokémon card, run through this:

**1. Check the pop report.** PSA's population report is free. Look up your exact card. If there are 8,000+ PSA 10s and the 10 sells for $120, walk away — you're paying $85 to join a crowded club.

**2. Compare raw vs. 10 honestly.** Pull *sold* listings (not asking prices) for your card raw and at PSA 10. Common trap: the raw price you remember from six months ago. Markets move — use last-30-day solds.

**3. Grade the card, not the memory.** Pull the card from the binder. Check centering front *and* back (Pokémon back centering fails more than you'd think), run a light across the holo for scratches, check the corners with magnification. Modern cards get 10s often — but "often" still means maybe 30–40% of pack-fresh copies.

**4. Do the break-even.** Raw price + ~$85 all-in = your break-even sale. If PSA 9s sell below that number, you're all-in on a 10. Make sure you're comfortable with that bet.

## Which Pokémon cards are usually worth grading?

- **Vintage holos in clean condition** (Base Set, Jungle, Fossil): the nostalgia premium is real and populations of true 10s are low relative to demand
- **Modern alt arts / SIRs** with strong multipliers: Umbreon VMAX alt art is the poster child — $420 raw to $980 at PSA 10
- **Trophy and promo cards**: low supply by design
- **Sealed-era grails** you genuinely believe are gem mint

## Which Pokémon cards are usually NOT worth grading?

- **Bulk holos and commons**: a $12 Pikachu that 10s at $210 sounds profitable until you weight the grade probabilities — and the pop report shows thousands of 10s
- **Cards with whitening or soft corners**: you're paying $85 for a PSA 8 worth less than the raw card was
- **Anything you're grading "just to see"**: curiosity grading is the most expensive habit in the hobby. $85 per lesson adds up fast
- **Heavily printed modern Vs and exs** with 10 prices under $150

## The submission strategy that works

Serious Pokémon submitters don't send cards one by one. They:

1. **Triage ruthlessly** — run 50 candidates through the calculator, submit the 8–10 with the best expected value
2. **Batch at Standard tier** — $59.99 is already steep; don't pay for speed unless the card is time-sensitive
3. **Track everything** — what you submitted, what came back, what it sold for. Your personal grading accuracy is the most valuable data you own
4. **Sell the 9s fast** — if a card 9s and the math only worked at 10, sell the 9 immediately and redeploy the capital

## Bottom line

Pokémon grading in 2026 is a sniper's game, not a shotgun's. The collectors making money aren't grading more — they're grading *better*, running the numbers on every card and submitting only where the multiplier, the condition, and the population all line up. Run your binder through the WorthASlab calculator before your next submission. Five seconds per card beats $85 of hindsight.

## Set-by-set quick scan (modern)

Not all sets are created equal for grading. A rough tier list for modern Pokémon:

**Tier S (grade aggressively):** Sets with chase alt arts and strong sustained demand — Evolving Skies, Crown Zenith, 151. The multipliers here are the market's best, and pop reports, while growing, haven't caught demand.

**Tier A (selective):** Most Sword & Shield and Scarlet & Violet sets. Plenty of submittable cards, but you must check each one — the print runs are large and the pop reports show it.

**Tier B (rarely):** Heavily opened sets with flat premiums — many special sets where everything was pulled by everyone. The occasional low-pop 10 still pays, but the hit rate is low.

**Vintage (always evaluate):** Base Set through Neo era. Different economics entirely — scarcity is real, nostalgia demand is permanent, and even PSA 8s and 9s carry premiums that modern cards can't touch. Condition is the whole ballgame here; most vintage fails on wear, not on math.

## When the pop report lies (or misleads)

The population report is essential, but read it critically:

- **Cracked and resubmitted cards** inflate pops — the same card can appear as a 9 and later a 10. Total graded counts overstate unique cards.
- **Qualifier-era cards** (OC, ST, PD qualifiers) muddy grade comparisons — a PSA 9(OC) is a different asset than a straight 9.
- **Growing pops aren't always bad.** If demand grows faster than supply — and for top Pokémon chase cards it has — rising pops don't compress premiums. Watch the *price trend* alongside the pop trend. Flat prices + rising pops = saturation warning. Rising prices + rising pops = healthy demand.

## Grading for the PC vs grading for profit

Two different games, two different filters:

**For profit:** everything in this guide applies at full strictness. The five-point filter, the pop report, the expected-value math. Ruthless.

**For the PC:** relax the financial filter but keep the condition filter. There's no point paying $85 to slab a card that comes back a 6 — even for the binder, a clean raw copy in a magnetic holder looks better than a low-grade slab. Grade your favorites, but grade *clean* favorites. And consider CGC for PC slabs where resale premiums don't matter — the money you save funds more of the hobby you actually enjoy.
