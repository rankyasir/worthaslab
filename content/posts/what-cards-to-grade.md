"What should I get graded?" is the wrong question. The right question is: "which of my cards survive a five-point filter?" Professional submitters don't have better eyes than you — they have a better filter. Here's theirs.

## The five-point filter

A card earns a submission only if it passes ALL five:

**1. Value floor.** Raw value of roughly $40 or more. Below this, the $59.99 minimum tier plus ~$25 in shipping and supplies makes profitability nearly impossible. Exceptions exist (low-pop vintage), but they are rare enough to treat as guilty until proven innocent.

**2. Slab multiplier of 2.5x+.** Divide the PSA 10 sold price by the raw sold price. Under 2.5x, the grading cost eats the margin at 2026 prices. At 5x+, you're in prime territory.

**3. Realistic gem-mint condition.** Pack-fresh isn't enough — it needs to be pack-perfect: centering within tolerance front and back, sharp corners, clean edges, flawless holo surface. If your honest assessment is "likely 9," run the numbers at the 9 price. If those numbers fail, the card fails.

**4. Population supports the premium.** Check the PSA pop report. A card with 20,000 PSA 10s needs enormous sustained demand to hold its premium. A card with 300 PSA 10s and a cult following is a different animal. Low pop relative to demand is where the real money lives.

**5. Dollar upside clears $100.** After all-in costs (~$85), you want at least $100 of expected profit at your likely grade — enough to justify months of waiting and the grading risk. Thin margins get eaten by variance: one grade lower than hoped and you're underwater.

## Cards that usually pass

**Vintage holos in clean condition.** Base Set, Jungle, Fossil, Team Rocket holos — the nostalgia bid is permanent, true gem-mint copies are genuinely scarce (kids played with these in 1999), and the multipliers are healthy. A clean raw copy is always worth evaluating.

**Modern alt arts and special illustration rares.** The market has spoken: it pays real premiums for these at PSA 10. Umbreon VMAX alt art ($420 raw → $980 PSA 10) is the archetype. Not every alt art qualifies — check the multiplier — but the category is the most fertile hunting ground in modern.

**Star rookie cards (sports).** Graded rookie cards of established stars carry durable premiums. The key word is *established* — grading a prospect's rookie is speculation on the player, not just the card. Price that risk accordingly.

**Trophy, promo, and low-print cards.** Supply-constrained by design. Even modest demand creates strong multipliers because the pop reports stay tiny.

**Your personal grails (with eyes open).** Grading isn't always about profit. If slabbing your favorite card brings you joy and you can afford the $85, that's a legitimate reason — just don't confuse it with investing.

## Cards that usually fail

**Bulk holos and modern commons.** The math is brutal and final. A $12 card needs a $210 PSA 10 just to clear $113 of theoretical profit — and the pop report will show you why that premium won't hold.

**Cards with visible wear.** Whitening on edges, soft corners, surface scratches — you're paying $85 for a PSA 7 or 8 worth less than the raw card. Be honest. Put it back.

**Heavily printed modern ultra-rares.** If everyone pulled it and everyone graded it, you're the marginal seller in a buyer's market. The pop report is your friend here.

**Cards in declining markets.** A player past his prime, a set rotating out of relevance, a character fading from the spotlight — grading locks you in for 4–5 months while the floor drops. Grade appreciating or stable assets, not melting ice cubes.

**Anything you're submitting "to see what happens."** Curiosity grading at $85 a pop is the most expensive education in the hobby. The calculator is free. Use it.

## The 10-minute triage method

Spread your candidates on a table. For each card, you need exactly three numbers: raw sold price, PSA 10 sold price, and your honest condition grade. That gives you the multiplier and the break-even in seconds. Sort by expected profit. Submit the top of the list until your budget runs out. Everything below the line goes back in the binder — no exceptions, no "but this one feels lucky."

Professionals aren't braver than you. They're just disciplined about the filter. Adopt it, and your submission ROI will change within a single batch.

## Category deep dives

**Sports cards.** The rookie-card economy runs on established stars. Grade rookies of players with real careers; be very careful with prospects — you're underwriting athletic outcomes, not just cardboard condition. Vintage sports (pre-1980) in any clean condition is almost always worth evaluating: supply is fixed and nostalgia demand doesn't fade.

**Magic: The Gathering.** The Reserved List is the whole story — cards that will never be reprinted have permanent supply caps. Alpha/Beta/early Unlimited in clean condition are grading royalty. Modern MTG is trickier: smaller collector base than Pokémon, thinner markets, wider spreads. Only the true staples and premium treatments clear the bar.

**Yu-Gi-Oh!** First-edition printings of iconic cards (Blue-Eyes, Dark Magician, Exodia pieces) carry the premiums; unlimited printings usually don't. The market is thinner than Pokémon's, so check *sold* volume, not just prices — a $700 PSA 10 that sells twice a year is a different asset than one selling weekly.

**Non-sport / entertainment.** Marvel, Star Wars, wrestling, F1 — passionate niches with real money, but thinner data. The filter still works; just demand more margin for the illiquidity. If only three PSA 10s sold last year, your "comp" is a guess wearing a suit.

## Revisit quarterly

Your filter isn't a tattoo — it's a living document. Every quarter, update three inputs:

1. **Tier prices.** PSA reprices; your $85 all-in assumption drifts. Refresh it.
2. **Your hit rate.** 20+ submissions in? Your real 10-rate replaces your hoped-for one in every calculation.
3. **Market shifts.** A set that was hot cools off; a player retires; a new grader gains share. The cards that passed your filter last quarter might fail this one.

The collectors who treat grading as a system — filter, submit, track, audit, adjust — compound their edge over time. The ones who treat it as vibes donate $85 at a time to the learning curve. You've now got the system. Use it.
